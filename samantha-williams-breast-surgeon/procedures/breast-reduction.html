Breast reduction

Reducing the size and weight of the breasts, with the breast reshaped and lifted in the same operation. Performed by Miss Samantha Williams at BMI The Ridgeway Hospital, Swindon.

Large breasts are often a physical problem rather than a cosmetic one: neck and shoulder pain, grooves from bra straps, rashes under the breasts, difficulty exercising and finding clothes that fit. Breast reduction (reduction mammoplasty) removes excess tissue, fat and skin and reshapes what remains into a smaller, lighter breast that sits higher on the chest.

Who it suits

Reduction suits women whose breast size causes physical symptoms, limits activity, or is out of proportion with the rest of their body. The result lasts best when your weight is stable, because a significant change in weight afterwards changes breast size again. Reduction can affect the ability to breastfeed, so if you are planning children it is worth discussing timing. Smoking increases the risk of wound-healing problems considerably, and Miss Williams will ask you to stop well before surgery.

Miss Williams uses reduction techniques routinely in cancer surgery as well as in cosmetic practice. The shape and position of the breast you are left with matters as much as the amount removed.

The consultation

Miss Williams discusses your symptoms, examines and measures you, and goes through:

  • How much smaller. Described in terms of proportion and the look you want. A precise cup size cannot be promised.
  • Nipple position. The nipple and areola are lifted to a higher position, and the areola can be made smaller if you wish.
  • The scar pattern. Most reductions use an anchor-shaped scar: around the areola, vertically down, and along the fold. A shorter vertical-only scar is possible in some cases.
  • Screening. If you are over 40 or have a family history of breast cancer, a mammogram before surgery may be recommended.

You receive a written plan, a cooling-off period of at least two weeks, and the offer of a further consultation before deciding.

The operation

Reduction is carried out under general anaesthetic and usually takes two to three hours. Excess tissue, fat and skin are removed, the remaining breast is reshaped and supported, and the nipple is moved upward while staying attached to its blood and nerve supply. Dissolvable stitches are used under the skin. Drains are sometimes placed for the first day. Most patients stay one night.

The tissue removed is sent for routine examination by a pathologist, which is standard practice in breast surgery.

Recovery

Most women find the first days more manageable than they expected. Discomfort is usually controlled with simple painkillers, and the relief of weight is often felt straight away. A supportive bra is worn around the clock for about six weeks. Desk-based work is usually possible after two weeks, driving once you can do an emergency stop comfortably, and full exercise from about six weeks.

Swelling settles over the first couple of months, and the breasts soften and settle into their final shape over three to six months. Scars are pink and raised at first and continue to fade and flatten for 12 to 18 months. Follow-up is usually at one week, six weeks and six months.

Risks

  • Scarring. Reduction leaves permanent scars. They usually fade well, but some people form thicker or wider scars, and this cannot be fully predicted.
  • Wound healing. Small areas of delayed healing, particularly where the vertical scar meets the fold, are fairly common and usually heal with dressings.
  • Changes in nipple sensation. Numbness or increased sensitivity is common early on and permanent in a minority. Rarely, the blood supply to the nipple is compromised and part or all of it can be lost.
  • Fat necrosis: firm lumps where fatty tissue has not healed well. These usually settle, and occasionally need removal.
  • Asymmetry, or a result larger or smaller than hoped, which occasionally needs adjustment.
  • Breastfeeding may be reduced or not possible afterwards.
  • Bleeding, infection, blood clots and anaesthetic risks, all higher in smokers and with a high BMI.

Questions patients ask

What size will I be afterwards?

The approximate reduction is agreed at the consultation, but an exact cup size cannot be promised. Bra sizing varies between brands and the final result depends on your tissue and how it heals. The aim is a breast in proportion with your build that relieves your symptoms.

Will reduction help my back and neck pain?

Many women report considerable relief of neck, shoulder and upper back discomfort after reduction, and it is one of the main reasons people choose the operation. It will not resolve pain that has another cause, and Miss Williams will discuss this if it applies to you.

Will I be able to breastfeed?

Possibly, but it is less likely than before surgery because some milk ducts are divided. If breastfeeding in future matters to you, raise it at the consultation.

Is breast reduction covered by insurance or the NHS?

Private insurers generally treat reduction as cosmetic unless there are specific medical grounds, and NHS funding is limited and varies by area. Most private reductions are self-funded. Costs are set out at the consultation.

How painful is the recovery?

Most patients describe soreness and tightness rather than severe pain, managed with simple painkillers for the first week or so.
