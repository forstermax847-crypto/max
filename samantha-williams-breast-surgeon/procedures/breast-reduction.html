Cosmetic Breast Surgery

Breast reduction

Removing weight and volume to relieve discomfort and restore proportion — while reshaping and lifting the breast at the same time. Performed by Miss Samantha Williams at BMI The Ridgeway Hospital, Swindon.

For many women, large breasts are not a cosmetic concern but a daily physical one: neck and shoulder pain, grooves from bra straps, rashes beneath the breasts, difficulty exercising and finding clothes that fit. Breast reduction (reduction mammoplasty) removes excess tissue, fat and skin, and reshapes what remains into a smaller, lighter breast that sits higher on the chest. Patients consistently describe it as one of the most life-changing operations they have had.

Is reduction right for you?

Breast reduction suits women whose breast size causes physical symptoms, limits activity or feels out of proportion with the rest of their body. Results are best and most lasting when you are at, or close to, a stable weight you can maintain, because significant weight change afterwards alters breast size again. If you are planning to breastfeed in future it is worth discussing timing, as reduction can affect the ability to breastfeed. Smoking markedly increases the risk of wound-healing problems, so Miss Williams will ask you to stop well before surgery.

As an oncoplastic surgeon, Miss Williams performs reduction techniques routinely in cancer surgery as well as cosmetic practice. That experience matters here: reduction is as much about the shape and position of the breast you are left with as about the volume removed.

Your consultation

Expect a thorough, unhurried appointment. Miss Williams will discuss your symptoms, examine and measure you, and talk through:

  • How much smaller — described in terms of proportion and the look you want rather than a promised cup size, which cannot be guaranteed.
  • Nipple position — the nipple and areola are lifted to a more youthful position and the areola can be made smaller if you wish.
  • The scar pattern — most reductions use an anchor-shaped scar (around the areola, vertically down, and along the fold). A shorter "lollipop" scar is possible in some cases.
  • Screening — if you are over 40, or have a family history, a mammogram before surgery may be recommended.

You will receive a written plan, a cooling-off period of at least two weeks, and the offer of a further consultation before deciding.

The operation

Reduction is carried out under general anaesthetic and typically takes two to three hours. Excess tissue, fat and skin are removed, the remaining breast is reshaped and supported, and the nipple is moved upward while staying attached to its own blood and nerve supply. Fine dissolvable stitches are used beneath the skin. Small drains are sometimes placed for the first day. You will usually stay one night.

All tissue removed is sent for routine examination by a pathologist — standard practice in breast surgery, and reassuring to have done.

Recovery

Most women are surprised by how manageable the first days are; discomfort is usually controlled with simple pain relief and the relief of weight is often felt straight away. You will wear a supportive bra around the clock for about six weeks. Desk-based work is usually possible after two weeks, driving once you can perform an emergency stop comfortably, and full exercise — including running and anything involving the chest — from around six weeks.

Swelling settles over the first couple of months and the breasts soften and "drop" into their final shape over three to six months. Scars are pink and raised at first and continue to fade and flatten for 12–18 months. Follow-up is typically at one week, six weeks and six months.

Risks and things to consider

  • Scarring — reduction leaves permanent scars. They usually fade well, but some people form thicker or wider scars and this cannot be fully predicted.
  • Wound healing — small areas of delayed healing, particularly where the vertical scar meets the fold, are relatively common and usually heal with dressings.
  • Changes in nipple sensation — numbness or increased sensitivity is common early on; in a minority it is permanent. Rarely, the nipple's blood supply is compromised and part or all of it can be lost.
  • Fat necrosis — firm lumps where fatty tissue has not healed well; usually settle, occasionally need removal.
  • Asymmetry and shape — small differences between sides, or a result larger or smaller than hoped, can occur and occasionally need adjustment.
  • Breastfeeding — may be reduced or not possible after reduction.
  • General risks — bleeding, infection, blood clots and anaesthetic risks, all higher in smokers and with a high BMI.

Frequently asked questions

What size will I be afterwards?

Miss Williams will agree the approximate reduction with you at consultation, but a precise cup size cannot be promised — bra sizing varies between brands and the final result depends on your tissue and how it heals. The aim is a breast in proportion with your frame that relieves your symptoms.

Will reduction cure my back and neck pain?

Many women report significant relief of neck, shoulder and upper back discomfort after reduction, and it is one of the main reasons people choose the operation. It cannot be guaranteed to resolve pain that has other causes, which is something Miss Williams will discuss honestly if it applies to you.

Will I be able to breastfeed?

Possibly, but it is less likely than before surgery because some milk ducts are divided. If future breastfeeding matters to you, raise it at consultation; it may affect the timing or technique recommended.

Is breast reduction covered by insurance or the NHS?

Reduction is generally treated as cosmetic by private insurers unless there are specific medical grounds, and NHS funding is limited and varies by area. Most private reductions are self-funded; costs are set out clearly at consultation.

How painful is the recovery?

Most patients describe soreness and tightness rather than severe pain, managed with simple painkillers for the first week or so. The sensation of lighter breasts is often immediate, which many women find outweighs the discomfort.
