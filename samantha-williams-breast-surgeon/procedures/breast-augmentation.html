Cosmetic breast surgery

Breast augmentation

Breast enlargement with implants, performed by Miss Samantha Williams at BMI The Ridgeway Hospital, Swindon.

Breast augmentation uses an implant to add volume, improve shape or restore fullness lost after pregnancy, breastfeeding or weight loss. It is the most common cosmetic breast operation in the UK. The decision that most affects how natural the result looks is the size and position of the implant, and that is where most of the consultation is spent.

Who it suits

Women consider augmentation for different reasons: breasts that have always been small for their build, volume lost after children or weight change, or a noticeable difference between the two sides. Good candidates are in reasonable general health, at a stable weight, not pregnant or breastfeeding, and clear about what they want to change.

An implant adds volume but does not lift. If the nipple sits low or the breast has dropped, an implant alone tends to make this more obvious. In that case Miss Williams may suggest an uplift, on its own or combined with an implant, and will explain the trade-offs of each.

The consultation

Miss Williams takes a full history, examines and measures you, and asks what you are hoping for. You try implant sizers in a bra to see how different volumes look on your frame. The main decisions are:

  • Size. Chosen from your chest width and the amount of breast tissue you have, not from a cup size. Miss Williams will say if the size you have in mind is likely to look artificial or cause problems later.
  • Implant type. Round or anatomical (teardrop) shape, surface finish, and profile.
  • Placement. In front of the chest muscle, or partly behind it (dual plane). Placement behind the muscle gives a more natural upper breast in slimmer patients.
  • Incision. Usually in the fold beneath the breast, where the scar is least visible.

You leave with a written plan. For cosmetic surgery there is a cooling-off period of at least two weeks before anything is booked, in line with General Medical Council guidance, and a second consultation is available if you want one.

The operation

Augmentation is performed under general anaesthetic with a consultant anaesthetist and usually takes between one and one and a half hours. A pocket is made behind the breast tissue or partly beneath the pectoral muscle, the implant is placed, and the incision is closed with dissolvable stitches under the skin. You wake in a supportive bra. Most patients go home the same day or the following morning.

Details of the implants are recorded in the Breast and Cosmetic Implant Registry, and you are given an implant card for your own records.

Recovery

The breasts feel tight, swollen and tender for the first week and settle after that. Simple painkillers are usually enough. Most patients are back at a desk job within one to two weeks and can drive once they can do an emergency stop comfortably, typically one to two weeks (check your insurer's rules). A supportive bra is worn day and night for about six weeks, with no lifting, upper-body exercise or swimming in that time.

Implants sit high and feel firm at first. They soften and settle over two to three months, and the final result is assessed at about six months. Scars are pink and slightly raised initially and fade over 12 to 18 months. Follow-up is usually at one week, six weeks and six months.

Risks

Augmentation is a well-established operation, but it is surgery, and implants do not last a lifetime. Miss Williams goes through the following in detail before you decide.

  • Bleeding, infection, delayed wound healing and anaesthetic risks. Smoking increases all of these.
  • Changes in nipple or breast sensation, usually temporary, occasionally permanent.
  • Capsular contracture: the scar tissue that forms around any implant can tighten over time, making the breast feel firm or look distorted. It may need further surgery.
  • Rippling, visible implant edges or rotation, more likely in slimmer patients with less natural tissue.
  • Asymmetry. No two breasts are identical before surgery, and small differences can remain or appear afterwards.
  • Implant rupture or wear. Many women need further surgery at some point to replace or remove implants.
  • Breast implant-associated anaplastic large cell lymphoma (BIA-ALCL), a rare cancer of the immune system linked mainly to textured implants, and the group of symptoms some women describe as breast implant illness. Both are discussed at the consultation.
  • Breastfeeding is usually still possible but cannot be guaranteed. Implants can make mammograms harder to read, so you should always tell a screening unit that you have them.

Questions patients ask

How is the implant size chosen?

From your measurements, your existing breast tissue and the look you describe, rather than from a cup size, which varies between brands. Trying sizers in a bra at the consultation gives a realistic sense of each volume. Miss Williams will advise on the range that suits your frame.

Will the implants look natural?

That depends mainly on choosing a size in proportion to your chest and placing the implant correctly for your tissue. Where the breast has dropped, an uplift may be needed as well. Results vary between patients and cannot be guaranteed.

Do implants need replacing?

Not on a fixed schedule, but they are not designed to last for life. Many women have further surgery at some point because of changes in the breast, capsular contracture, rupture or a change in preference. It is sensible to plan on the basis that you may need another operation in future.

Can I breastfeed after augmentation?

Most women can. An incision in the breast fold and placement beneath the muscle both help to protect the milk ducts and nerves, but breastfeeding cannot be guaranteed after any breast surgery.

Does augmentation affect breast screening?

Implants can hide part of the breast on a mammogram, so the screening unit takes extra views when they know you have them. Always tell them.

When can I exercise?

Gentle walking from the first few days, light lower-body exercise from about two weeks, and running, swimming and upper-body work from about six weeks, once you have been checked at follow-up.
