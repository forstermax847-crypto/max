Breast cancer care

Breast reconstruction

Rebuilding the shape of the breast after a mastectomy or cancer surgery, at the time of the cancer operation or later, with an implant or your own tissue. Planned and performed by Miss Samantha Williams, working with plastic surgery colleagues where needed.

Reconstruction is a set of options rather than a single operation. The right one depends on your body, your treatment, your health, and how you feel about scars, recovery and the possibility of further surgery. The consultation is about setting out what each option can and cannot achieve so that you can decide.

Immediate or delayed

Immediate reconstruction is done in the same operation as the mastectomy, so you do not wake without a breast shape, and the breast skin can often be kept. Delayed reconstruction is done months or years later, sometimes because radiotherapy is planned (which can affect an implant reconstruction), and sometimes because you would rather deal with one thing at a time. Neither is better in general; both are discussed with you and with the multidisciplinary team.

The main options

Implant-based reconstruction

A silicone implant, sometimes placed after a temporary expander has gradually stretched the skin, and often supported by a mesh. It is the shorter operation with the quicker recovery and no scars elsewhere on the body. It carries the long-term considerations of any implant: capsular contracture (more likely after radiotherapy), the possibility of replacement, and a result that tends to feel firmer and sit higher than a natural breast.

Own-tissue reconstruction (DIEP flap)

Skin and fat from the lower abdomen are moved to the chest and reconnected to blood vessels there under a microscope. The result is a soft, warm breast that ages with you, and a flatter abdomen with a scar similar to a tummy tuck. It is a longer operation with a longer recovery, performed jointly with Miss Williams's plastic surgery colleagues. It is usually the most durable option, particularly if radiotherapy is part of your treatment.

Lipofilling (fat transfer)

Fat is taken from elsewhere on the body by liposuction and injected to smooth contours, soften the edge of an implant, or improve tissue quality after radiotherapy. It is a day-case procedure and often needs to be repeated two or three times, because some of the transferred fat does not survive.

Symmetrising surgery and nipple reconstruction

The other breast can be reduced, lifted or augmented so that the two match. Once the shape has settled, a nipple can be reconstructed from local skin and the areola recreated by specialist tattooing. These stages are planned with you from the outset.

Choosing between them

The discussion covers whether radiotherapy is planned or has already been given, your general health and body shape, whether you smoke, how much time you can give to recovery, your feelings about scars elsewhere on your body, and how important it is to you to avoid an implant. There is rarely one right answer. Miss Williams gives her opinion, and the decision is yours. Second opinions and revision of previous reconstructions are a regular part of her practice.

Recovery

After implant reconstruction most patients are home within a day or two, back to light activity within a couple of weeks and recovered in about four to six weeks. DIEP reconstruction involves a hospital stay of several days and a recovery of six to eight weeks or more. In both cases follow-up is structured and the team can be contacted between appointments. The final appearance takes months to settle, and the later stages (symmetrising, lipofilling, nipple reconstruction) are spaced accordingly.

Risks

  • Infection and wound-healing problems. With implants, infection can occasionally mean the implant has to be removed.
  • Capsular contracture around an implant, particularly after radiotherapy, which may need further surgery.
  • With DIEP reconstruction: partial or, rarely, complete loss of the transferred tissue, fat necrosis, and weakness or bulging of the abdominal wall at the donor site.
  • Seroma, numbness of the reconstructed breast (reconstructions do not regain normal sensation), and asymmetry.
  • Further operations. Most reconstructions involve more than one procedure over time, planned or otherwise.
  • Bleeding, blood clots and anaesthetic risks, higher in smokers and with a high BMI.

Questions patients ask

Will a reconstructed breast look and feel like my own?

A good reconstruction restores shape and symmetry in clothes and often without, but it is not the same as the breast you had. Sensation is reduced or absent, and implants feel firmer than natural tissue. Own-tissue reconstruction comes closest to a natural feel.

Does radiotherapy change which reconstruction I should have?

Often, yes. Radiotherapy increases the risk of complications and hardening around an implant, so if it is planned Miss Williams may recommend delaying reconstruction or choosing own tissue.

How many operations will I need?

It varies. Some implant reconstructions are completed in one stage; many involve two or three procedures over a year or more, including symmetrising the other side and nipple reconstruction. The likely stages for your chosen option are set out at the start.

Can I have reconstruction years after my mastectomy?

Yes. Delayed reconstruction is possible many years later. Previous radiotherapy and your current health affect which options are suitable.

Is reconstruction covered by insurance?

Reconstruction after breast cancer, including symmetrising surgery to the other breast, is usually covered by private medical insurance, subject to your policy and pre-authorisation. It is also available on the NHS.
