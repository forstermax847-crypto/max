Breast Health

What to expect at a breast lump assessment

Finding a lump is frightening, and not knowing what happens next makes it worse. Here is what a one-stop assessment actually involves, step by step.

By Miss Samantha WilliamsConsultant Oncoplastic Breast SurgeonAbout 5 minutes
{{draft_notice}}

If you have found a lump, noticed a change, or your GP has suggested you be seen, the single most useful thing to know is this: the great majority of breast lumps are benign. Cysts, fibroadenomas and ordinary glandular tissue account for most of what we see in clinic. But the only way to be sure is a proper assessment — and the sooner it happens, the sooner the worrying stops.

Why "triple assessment"?

Breast clinics in the UK work to a standard called triple assessment. It means a lump is examined in three ways — by a clinician, by imaging, and (if needed) by taking a sample of cells or tissue — and the three results are read together. Each test on its own can miss things; combined, they are highly accurate. A good private clinic follows exactly the same standard as an NHS one; the difference is usually speed, and seeing the same consultant throughout.

Before the appointment

You do not need a GP referral to book a self-pay appointment, though if you are using insurance your insurer will usually want one. Bring any previous breast imaging reports, a list of your medications, and a note of when you first noticed the change. Wearing a top and bottom rather than a dress makes examination easier. Bringing someone with you is welcome — a second pair of ears is useful in any medical appointment, and more so when you are anxious.

Step one: the conversation and examination

The appointment begins with questions: what you have noticed, when, whether it changes with your cycle, any pain or nipple discharge, your general health, medications and family history. Then a careful examination of both breasts and the lymph nodes under the arms and in the neck. A chaperone is always offered. This part usually takes ten to fifteen minutes and tells an experienced surgeon a great deal — but it is never the whole answer, which is why imaging follows.

Step two: imaging

The type of scan depends mainly on your age, because breast tissue changes over time. In younger women the breast is denser, and ultrasound shows lumps more clearly; from about forty, a mammogram is usually added. The imaging is carried out by specialist breast radiologists in the hospital's imaging department and, in a one-stop clinic, on the same day as your consultation. Ultrasound is painless; a mammogram involves brief compression that most people find uncomfortable rather than painful.

Step three: biopsy, if it is needed

Many lumps can be confidently diagnosed on imaging alone — a simple cyst, for example, has a characteristic appearance and may simply be drained there and then. If the imaging shows something that needs characterising, a core biopsy is taken: after local anaesthetic, a fine needle guided by ultrasound removes a tiny sample of tissue. It takes a few minutes, and most people describe pressure and a clicking sound rather than pain. Some bruising afterwards is normal.

Getting your results

Imaging results are explained to you before you leave — you will not go home not knowing what the scan showed. Biopsy results come back from the laboratory in around a week, and you will be given them in person, or by phone if you prefer, by the same surgeon who saw you. If everything is benign, you will be told what, if anything, to keep an eye on. If a benign lump is large, growing or simply bothering you, it can be removed. And if the result is cancer, you will hear it from the person who is going to look after you, with a plan and the time to understand it.

Questions worth asking

  • What did the scan show, and how confident are you in that?
  • If a biopsy is needed, when and how will I get the result?
  • Is there anything I should look out for in the meantime?
  • If this is benign, do I need any follow-up?
  • How should I check my breasts from now on?

The one piece of advice

Do not wait to see whether a lump goes away. Most will turn out to be nothing to worry about — but the ones that are not are far easier to treat when found early, and the weeks spent hoping are weeks of needless anxiety. If something has changed, have it looked at.
