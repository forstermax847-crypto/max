Breast Cancer Care

Breast reconstruction

Rebuilding the shape of the breast after a mastectomy or cancer surgery — at the same time as the cancer operation or later, with an implant or your own tissue. Planned and performed by Miss Samantha Williams, working with plastic surgery colleagues where needed.

Reconstruction is not one operation but a set of options, and the right one is personal: it depends on your body, your treatment, your health and how you feel about scars, recovery and future surgery. Miss Williams's aim is a reconstruction that lets you feel like yourself again — and an honest conversation beforehand about what each option can and cannot achieve.

Immediate or delayed?

Immediate reconstruction is performed in the same operation as the mastectomy, so you never wake without a breast shape and the breast skin can often be preserved. Delayed reconstruction is carried out months or years later — sometimes because radiotherapy is planned (which can affect an implant reconstruction), sometimes because you would rather focus on one thing at a time. Neither is "better"; both are discussed with you and with the multidisciplinary team.

The main options

Implant-based reconstruction

A silicone implant, sometimes placed after a temporary expander has gradually stretched the skin, often supported by a mesh. It is the shorter operation with the quicker recovery and no scars elsewhere on the body. It does carry the long-term considerations of any implant — capsular contracture (more likely after radiotherapy), the possibility of needing replacement, and a result that tends to feel firmer and sit higher than a natural breast.

Own-tissue (autologous) reconstruction — DIEP flap

Skin and fat from the lower abdomen are moved to the chest and reconnected to blood vessels there under a microscope, creating a soft, warm breast that ages naturally with you — and a flatter abdomen, with a scar similar to a tummy tuck. It is a longer operation with a longer recovery and is performed jointly with Miss Williams's plastic surgery colleagues. It is usually the most durable option, particularly if radiotherapy is part of your treatment.

Lipofilling (fat transfer)

Fat is taken from elsewhere on the body by liposuction and injected to smooth contours, soften the edge of an implant or improve tissue quality after radiotherapy. It is a day-case procedure and often needs to be repeated two or three times to build up the effect, as some of the transferred fat does not survive.

Symmetrising surgery and finishing touches

The other breast can be reduced, lifted or augmented so the two match. Once the shape has settled, a nipple can be reconstructed from local skin and the areola recreated with specialist tattooing. Miss Williams plans these stages with you from the outset so you know what the whole journey looks like.

Choosing between them

The discussion will cover whether radiotherapy is planned or has been given, your general health and body shape, whether you smoke, how much time you can give to recovery, your feelings about scars elsewhere on your body, and how important it is to you to avoid an implant. There is rarely a single right answer, and Miss Williams will give you her honest opinion while respecting that the decision is yours. Second opinions and revision of previous reconstructions are a regular part of her practice.

Recovery

After implant reconstruction most patients are home in a day or two, back to light activity within a couple of weeks and fully recovered in around four to six weeks. DIEP reconstruction involves a hospital stay of several days and a recovery of six to eight weeks or more, with early mobilisation and a gradual return to activity. Whichever route you take, follow-up is structured and the team is available between appointments. The final appearance of any reconstruction takes months to settle, and the later stages — symmetrising, lipofilling, nipple reconstruction — are spaced out accordingly.

Risks and things to consider

  • Infection and wound-healing problems — more significant with implants, where infection can occasionally mean the implant has to be removed.
  • Capsular contracture around an implant, particularly after radiotherapy, which may require further surgery.
  • Flap problems — with DIEP reconstruction, partial or (rarely) complete loss of the transferred tissue, fat necrosis, and abdominal wall weakness or bulge at the donor site.
  • Seroma, numbness of the reconstructed breast (reconstructions do not regain normal sensation) and asymmetry.
  • Further operations — most reconstructions involve more than one procedure, planned or otherwise, over time.
  • General risks — bleeding, blood clots and anaesthetic risks, higher in smokers and with a high BMI.

Frequently asked questions

Will a reconstructed breast look and feel like my own?

A good reconstruction restores shape and symmetry in clothes and often without, but it is not the same as the breast you had: sensation is reduced or absent, and implants feel firmer than natural tissue. Own-tissue reconstruction comes closest to a natural feel. Being clear about this beforehand is part of a good result.

Does radiotherapy change which reconstruction I should have?

Often, yes. Radiotherapy increases the risk of complications and hardening around an implant, so if it is planned Miss Williams may recommend delaying reconstruction or choosing own tissue. It is one of the first things considered in planning.

How many operations will I need?

It varies. Some implant reconstructions are completed in one stage; many involve two or three procedures over a year or more, including symmetrising the other side and nipple reconstruction. Miss Williams will map out the likely stages for your chosen option at the start.

Can I have reconstruction years after my mastectomy?

Yes. Delayed reconstruction is possible many years later, and women who originally chose not to reconstruct sometimes change their minds. Previous radiotherapy and your current health will shape which options are suitable.

Is reconstruction covered by insurance?

Reconstruction after breast cancer, including symmetrising surgery to the other breast, is usually covered by private medical insurance, subject to your policy and pre-authorisation. It is also available on the NHS; Miss Williams works in both settings and can advise.
