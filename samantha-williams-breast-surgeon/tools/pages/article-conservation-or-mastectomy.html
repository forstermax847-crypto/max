Breast Cancer

Breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy?

One of the first and hardest questions after a diagnosis. What actually decides it — and why, for many women, the choice is wider than it used to be.

By Miss Samantha WilliamsConsultant Oncoplastic Breast SurgeonAbout 6 minutes
{{draft_notice}}

When breast cancer is diagnosed, surgery to remove it is usually the first step, and the question that follows is whether that means removing the lump or removing the breast. It is a question with medical answers and personal ones, and a good consultation makes room for both. This article explains the factors your surgeon is weighing, so that the conversation starts from the same place.

The two operations, briefly

Breast-conserving surgery — also called a wide local excision or lumpectomy — removes the cancer with a margin of healthy tissue around it and keeps the rest of the breast. It is almost always followed by radiotherapy to the breast. Mastectomy removes all of the breast tissue, with or without reconstruction, and radiotherapy is needed less often.

The most important thing to understand is that, for cancers where both are suitable, long-term survival is the same. Decades of evidence show that breast-conserving surgery plus radiotherapy is as effective as mastectomy at treating the cancer. The choice, where there is one, is not between a safe option and a risky one.

What decides which is recommended

Size — relative to the breast

It is not the size of the cancer on its own that matters, but its size relative to your breast. A three-centimetre cancer may be straightforward to remove from a large breast and very difficult to remove from a small one without leaving a significant dent. This is where oncoplastic techniques change the picture — more on those below.

How many, and where

Cancers in more than one part of the breast, or very extensive areas of pre-cancerous change on imaging, usually need a mastectomy to remove them completely. Position also matters: a cancer directly behind the nipple raises different questions from one at the edge of the breast.

Radiotherapy

Breast-conserving surgery relies on radiotherapy afterwards. If you have had radiotherapy to that breast before, have certain conditions that make it unsafe, or cannot commit to the course of daily treatments, mastectomy may be advised instead.

Genetics

Women carrying inherited gene changes such as BRCA1 or BRCA2 have a higher risk of further cancers, and some choose mastectomy — sometimes of both breasts — for that reason. Genetic testing may be offered before surgery if your history suggests it.

Your preference

Some women, offered both, choose mastectomy for peace of mind; others would do almost anything to keep their breast. Both are legitimate, and a surgeon's job is to make sure the choice is informed, not to make it for you.

How oncoplastic surgery widens the choice

Oncoplastic surgery combines cancer surgery with the techniques of plastic surgery. Its purpose is to remove larger cancers while still keeping the breast — and a breast shape worth keeping. Two approaches in particular mean that many women who would once have been told they needed a mastectomy now have an alternative.

Therapeutic mammoplasty uses a breast-reduction technique: the cancer is removed as part of a reduction and lift, and the other breast can be reduced to match. For women with larger breasts it often leaves a better shape than before the diagnosis.

Chest wall perforator flaps move a wedge of tissue from the side or underneath of the breast — kept alive on its own small blood vessels — into the space left where the cancer was. This fills the defect and maintains the breast's shape, and is particularly valuable in smaller breasts where a simple excision would leave an obvious hollow. Not every unit offers these techniques, so it is worth asking whether they are an option for you.

If mastectomy is the right operation

A recommendation of mastectomy is not a failure of the alternatives — for some cancers it is simply the right treatment. The modern version is often a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing operation with immediate reconstruction, so that you do not wake without a breast shape unless you choose to. Reconstruction can be with an implant or your own tissue, now or later, and is a decision in its own right.

Questions worth asking

  • Are both operations suitable for my cancer? If not, why not?
  • Would an oncoplastic technique let me keep my breast with a good shape?
  • What will my breast look like afterwards, and what can be done about the other side?
  • If I have conserving surgery, how likely is it that I would need a second operation?
  • Will I need radiotherapy either way?
  • If I need a mastectomy, what are my reconstruction options and when?
  • How long do I have to decide?

Take the time you need

Breast cancer is rarely an emergency in the sense of days, and a decision made with understanding is worth a week or two. Your case will be discussed by a full multidisciplinary team before any recommendation is made, and a second opinion is something any good surgeon will support rather than resent. The aim is not just to treat the cancer — it is to treat it in a way you can live well with afterwards.
