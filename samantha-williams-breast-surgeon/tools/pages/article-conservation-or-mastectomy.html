Breast cancer

Breast-conserving surgery or mastectomy?

What decides which operation is recommended, and why the choice is wider than it used to be.

Miss Samantha Williams, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon. About 6 minutes to read.

{{draft_notice}}

When breast cancer is diagnosed, surgery to remove it is usually the first step, and the question that follows is whether that means removing the lump or removing the breast. The question has medical answers and personal ones. This article explains what the surgeon is weighing, so that the conversation starts from the same place.

The two operations

Breast-conserving surgery, also called wide local excision or lumpectomy, removes the cancer with a margin of healthy tissue around it and keeps the rest of the breast. It is almost always followed by radiotherapy. Mastectomy removes all of the breast tissue, with or without reconstruction, and radiotherapy is needed less often.

For cancers where both operations are suitable, long-term survival is the same. Decades of evidence show that breast-conserving surgery with radiotherapy is as effective as mastectomy at treating the cancer. Where there is a choice, it is not between a safe option and a risky one.

What decides the recommendation

Size relative to the breast

What matters is the size of the cancer relative to the breast, not its size alone. A three-centimetre cancer may be straightforward to remove from a large breast and very difficult to remove from a small one without leaving a significant dent. This is where oncoplastic techniques change the picture.

Number and position

Cancers in more than one part of the breast, or very extensive areas of pre-cancerous change on imaging, usually need a mastectomy to remove them completely. A cancer directly behind the nipple raises different questions from one at the edge of the breast.

Radiotherapy

Breast-conserving surgery relies on radiotherapy afterwards. If you have had radiotherapy to that breast before, have a condition that makes it unsafe, or cannot commit to the course of daily treatments, mastectomy may be advised instead.

Genetics

Women with inherited gene changes such as BRCA1 or BRCA2 have a higher risk of further cancers, and some choose mastectomy, sometimes of both breasts, for that reason. Genetic testing may be offered before surgery if your history suggests it.

Your preference

Some women, offered both, choose mastectomy for peace of mind. Others want to keep their breast if it is at all possible. Both are reasonable. The surgeon's job is to make sure the choice is informed.

How oncoplastic surgery widens the choice

Oncoplastic surgery combines cancer surgery with the techniques of plastic surgery. Its purpose is to remove larger cancers while keeping the breast and a breast shape worth keeping. Two techniques in particular mean that many women who would once have been told they needed a mastectomy now have an alternative.

Therapeutic mammoplasty uses a breast-reduction technique. The cancer is removed as part of a reduction and lift, and the other breast can be reduced to match. For women with larger breasts it often leaves a better shape than before the diagnosis.

Chest wall perforator flaps move a wedge of tissue from the side or underneath of the breast, kept alive on its own small blood vessels, into the space left where the cancer was. This fills the defect and maintains the shape, and is particularly useful in smaller breasts where a simple excision would leave an obvious hollow. Not every unit offers these techniques, so it is worth asking whether they are an option for you.

If mastectomy is the right operation

A recommendation of mastectomy is not a failure of the alternatives. For some cancers it is the right treatment. The modern version is often a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing operation with immediate reconstruction, so that you do not wake without a breast shape unless you choose to. Reconstruction can be with an implant or your own tissue, now or later, and is a decision in its own right.

Questions worth asking

  • Are both operations suitable for my cancer? If not, why not?
  • Would an oncoplastic technique let me keep my breast with a good shape?
  • What will my breast look like afterwards, and what can be done about the other side?
  • If I have conserving surgery, how likely is it that I would need a second operation?
  • Will I need radiotherapy either way?
  • If I need a mastectomy, what are my reconstruction options and when?
  • How long do I have to decide?

Taking time to decide

Breast cancer is rarely an emergency measured in days, and a decision made with understanding is worth a week or two. Your case will be discussed by a multidisciplinary team before any recommendation is made, and a second opinion is something any good surgeon will support. The aim is to treat the cancer in a way you can live well with afterwards.
