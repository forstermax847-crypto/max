Recovering from breast reduction surgery

Patients often say the recovery was easier than they feared — but knowing what each stage looks like makes it easier still. A realistic picture of the first year.

By Miss Samantha WilliamsConsultant Oncoplastic Breast SurgeonAbout 6 minutes
{{draft_notice}}

Everyone heals at their own pace, and the timings below are typical rather than promised. But most women recovering from breast reduction follow a recognisable path, and the thing they most often tell me afterwards is that the relief of weight was felt almost immediately — before the soreness had even settled.

The first few days

You will usually stay in hospital for one night. Expect the breasts to feel tight, sore and heavy with dressings, and to feel tired — that is the anaesthetic and the body's response to surgery rather than anything to worry about. Simple painkillers taken regularly are usually enough; stronger ones are available if you need them in the first day or two. You will go home in a soft, supportive post-surgical bra, which you will wear day and night for about six weeks. Sleep propped up on pillows, on your back, for the first couple of weeks: it reduces swelling and keeps pressure off the wounds.

Move around the house from day one. Short, gentle walks help circulation and reduce the risk of clots. Keep your arms below shoulder height and avoid lifting anything heavier than a kettle — including small children and pets, who have a way of jumping.

Week one

Swelling and bruising peak around day three to five and then begin to ease. Dressings are checked at your first follow-up, typically around a week after surgery. Some numbness of the skin and nipples is normal at this stage. Showering is usually fine once dressings allow; baths and swimming wait until the wounds have fully healed. Many patients find they need very little pain relief by the end of the first week.

Weeks two and three

Most women return to desk-based or light work after about two weeks. You can drive again once you could perform an emergency stop without hesitation — usually around two weeks, but check your insurer's policy. Gentle lower-body exercise such as walking and a stationary bike is fine; nothing that bounces or works the chest. Small areas of delayed healing, most often where the vertical scar meets the fold under the breast, are not unusual at this stage and are managed with simple dressings — mention anything that concerns you.

Six weeks

This is the point at which most restrictions lift. After your six-week review you can usually return to full exercise — running, swimming, the gym, upper-body work — building up gradually, and switch from the surgical bra to a well-fitting, supportive everyday bra without underwiring for a further few weeks. Swelling has largely settled, though the breasts are still firmer and sit higher than they eventually will.

Three to six months

The breasts soften and settle into their final shape, dropping slightly into a more natural position. Sensation often continues to return over this period, sometimes with odd tingling or shooting feelings as nerves recover — a good sign, if an unusual one. This is when most patients feel the result has "become theirs". Your six-month review is usually when the outcome is formally assessed.

Scars over the first year

Reduction scars run around the areola, vertically down the breast and usually along the fold beneath it. They are red, raised and noticeable for the first few months, then gradually flatten and fade to pale lines over twelve to eighteen months. Keeping them out of the sun and massaging them with a simple moisturiser once fully healed helps. Silicone gel or tape can be used from about three weeks if you are prone to thicker scars. Some people scar more than others, and this cannot be fully predicted — but almost everyone finds the scars fade far more than they feared, and hidden entirely by a bra or bikini.

When to get in touch sooner

  • Increasing pain, swelling or firmness on one side, particularly in the first few days
  • Redness spreading from a wound, wound discharge, or a temperature
  • Any change in nipple colour
  • Calf pain or breathlessness

You will have the team's contact details before you go home, and you are never a nuisance for calling.

Making the result last

A reduction is permanent, but breasts still respond to life: significant weight gain, pregnancy and breastfeeding can all change them again. Keeping your weight stable and wearing a supportive bra for exercise are the two things that most help the result last.
