Breast Cancer Care

Breast cancer surgery

Oncoplastic surgery combines cancer surgery with plastic surgical technique in a single operation — removing the cancer completely while preserving the shape of the breast wherever it is safe to do so. Miss Samantha Williams, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon, Swindon.

A breast cancer diagnosis brings a great many decisions in a short space of time. Miss Williams's approach is to slow that down: to explain what the scans and biopsy actually show, to set out every surgical option honestly — including the ones she would not recommend — and to make sure that treating the cancer and caring about how you look afterwards are never in competition.

Your surgical options

Which operation is right depends on the size and position of the cancer relative to your breast, its type, what the imaging shows, any genetic factors, whether radiotherapy is planned, and — importantly — your own preferences. Miss Williams specialises in techniques that widen the choice beyond "lumpectomy or mastectomy".

Breast-conserving surgery

Also called a wide local excision or lumpectomy: the cancer is removed with a rim of healthy tissue around it, and the breast is reshaped so the result is as natural as possible. It is usually followed by radiotherapy. For suitable cancers, long-term survival is equivalent to mastectomy.

Therapeutic mammoplasty

For women with larger breasts, a breast-reduction technique is used to remove the cancer and reshape and lift the breast at the same time. It allows larger cancers to be removed while keeping the breast, often with a better cosmetic result than before, and the other breast can be reduced to match — either in the same operation or later.

Chest wall perforator flap reconstruction

One of Miss Williams's specialist interests. Tissue from the side or beneath the breast, kept alive on its own small blood vessels (known as LICAP, AICAP or TDAP flaps), is moved into the space left by removing the cancer. This fills the defect and maintains the breast's shape, and means many women with cancers that would once have required a mastectomy can keep their breast instead.

Mastectomy

Removal of the whole breast is still the right operation for some women — for large or multiple cancers, some inherited risks, or by preference. Where appropriate it can be done as a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction, so you never wake without a breast shape unless you choose to.

Lymph node surgery

Most patients have a sentinel lymph node biopsy during the same operation — removing the first one or two nodes the breast drains to, to check whether cancer has spread. If nodes are known to be involved, a more extensive axillary clearance or other treatment may be recommended.

How decisions are made

Every case is discussed at a multidisciplinary team meeting with breast radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and specialist nurses before a recommendation is made — this is how Miss Williams practises in both her NHS and private work. You will then sit down with her to go through the recommendation, the alternatives, and what each means for recovery, radiotherapy and appearance. Second opinions are welcomed, not resented.

Before and after surgery

You will have a pre-operative assessment, and if the cancer cannot be felt it is marked beforehand with a tiny wire or seed under imaging guidance so it can be located precisely. Surgery is under general anaesthetic with a consultant anaesthetist. Depending on the operation you will go home the same day or stay one or two nights; after a mastectomy a drain is usually in place for a few days.

Recovery varies with the operation: most women are moving about comfortably within days, back to light activity in one to two weeks and driving once comfortable. Gentle arm and shoulder exercises start early to maintain movement. The removed tissue and nodes are examined in the laboratory and the results — including whether the margins around the cancer are clear — are discussed with you at follow-up, typically one to two weeks after surgery, along with any further treatment such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy or hormone therapy.

Risks and things to consider

  • Need for further surgery — if the laboratory finds cancer cells at the edge of the removed tissue, a second operation to remove more tissue (or occasionally a mastectomy) is needed. This affects a minority of breast-conserving operations.
  • Seroma — fluid collecting under the wound, common after node surgery and mastectomy, sometimes needing to be drained in clinic.
  • Numbness of the breast skin or upper inner arm after node surgery, which may be permanent.
  • Lymphoedema — swelling of the arm, uncommon after sentinel node biopsy but a more significant risk after full axillary clearance.
  • Changes in breast shape, asymmetry, fat necrosis or indentation, particularly after radiotherapy.
  • Flap complications — with perforator flaps, partial loss of the moved tissue is uncommon but possible.
  • General risks — bleeding, infection, delayed healing, blood clots and anaesthetic risks.

Frequently asked questions

Will I need a mastectomy?

Many women do not. With oncoplastic techniques such as therapeutic mammoplasty and perforator flap reconstruction, cancers that would once have meant losing the breast can often be removed while keeping it. Whether that is safe for you depends on the size, position and type of cancer, and Miss Williams will explain the reasoning either way.

How quickly can I have surgery after diagnosis?

Once the multidisciplinary team has reviewed your results and you have decided on a plan, surgery can usually be arranged within a few weeks. Where other treatment such as chemotherapy is recommended first, the sequence will be explained clearly.

Will I need radiotherapy afterwards?

Radiotherapy is usually recommended after breast-conserving surgery to reduce the chance of the cancer returning in the breast, and sometimes after mastectomy. Your oncologist will advise; Miss Williams plans the surgery with any likely radiotherapy in mind.

What about the other breast?

If surgery changes the size or shape of one breast, the other can be adjusted to match — a reduction, uplift or augmentation — either during the same operation or once your treatment is complete. This "symmetrising" surgery is a normal part of oncoplastic care.

Can I have reconstruction at the same time as a mastectomy?

In many cases, yes — immediate reconstruction with an implant or, with plastic surgery colleagues, your own tissue. Sometimes delaying reconstruction until after radiotherapy gives a better result. The options are set out on the reconstruction page and discussed in full at consultation.

Is private breast cancer surgery covered by insurance?

Cancer surgery and treatment are usually covered by private medical insurance, subject to your policy and pre-authorisation. Miss Williams also treats NHS patients at Great Western Hospital, and can discuss the options if you are considering moving between the two.
