Breast cancer care

Breast cancer surgery

Oncoplastic surgery combines cancer surgery with plastic surgical techniques in one operation, so that the cancer is removed completely and the shape of the breast is preserved wherever that is safe. Miss Samantha Williams, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon, Swindon.

A diagnosis of breast cancer brings a lot of decisions in a short time. Miss Williams's approach is to explain what the scans and biopsy show, set out each surgical option including the ones she would not recommend, and make sure that treating the cancer and the appearance of the breast afterwards are considered together from the start.

The surgical options

Which operation is recommended depends on the size and position of the cancer relative to the breast, its type, what the imaging shows, any genetic factors, whether radiotherapy is planned, and your own preferences. Miss Williams specialises in techniques that widen the choice beyond a simple lumpectomy or a mastectomy.

Breast-conserving surgery

Also called a wide local excision or lumpectomy. The cancer is removed with a rim of healthy tissue around it and the breast is reshaped. It is usually followed by radiotherapy. For suitable cancers, long-term survival is the same as after mastectomy.

Therapeutic mammoplasty

For women with larger breasts, a breast-reduction technique is used to remove the cancer and to reshape and lift the breast in the same operation. It allows larger cancers to be removed while keeping the breast, and the other breast can be reduced to match, either at the same time or later.

Chest wall perforator flap reconstruction

One of Miss Williams's specialist interests. Tissue from the side or underneath of the breast, kept alive on its own small blood vessels (LICAP, AICAP or TDAP flaps), is moved into the space left by removing the cancer. This fills the defect and maintains the breast's shape, and means many women with cancers that would once have needed a mastectomy can keep their breast.

Mastectomy

Removal of the whole breast is still the right operation for some women, for large or multiple cancers, some inherited risks, or by preference. Where appropriate it can be done as a skin-sparing or nipple-sparing mastectomy with immediate reconstruction.

Lymph node surgery

Most patients have a sentinel lymph node biopsy during the same operation. The first one or two nodes the breast drains to are removed and checked for cancer. If nodes are known to be involved, a more extensive axillary clearance or other treatment may be recommended.

How decisions are made

Every case is discussed at a multidisciplinary team meeting with breast radiologists, pathologists, oncologists and specialist nurses before a recommendation is made. This is how Miss Williams works in both her NHS and private practice. You then go through the recommendation and the alternatives with her, including what each means for recovery, radiotherapy and appearance. Second opinions are welcome.

Before and after surgery

You have a pre-operative assessment. If the cancer cannot be felt, it is marked beforehand with a small wire or seed under imaging guidance so that it can be located precisely. Surgery is under general anaesthetic with a consultant anaesthetist. Depending on the operation you go home the same day or stay one or two nights. After a mastectomy a drain is usually in place for a few days.

Recovery varies with the operation. Most women are moving about comfortably within days, back to light activity in one to two weeks, and driving once comfortable. Gentle arm and shoulder exercises start early to keep movement. The removed tissue and nodes are examined in the laboratory. The results, including whether the margins around the cancer are clear, are discussed at follow-up, usually one to two weeks after surgery, along with any further treatment such as radiotherapy, chemotherapy or hormone therapy.

Risks

  • Further surgery. If the laboratory finds cancer cells at the edge of the removed tissue, a second operation to remove more tissue, or occasionally a mastectomy, is needed. This affects a minority of breast-conserving operations.
  • Seroma: fluid collecting under the wound. Common after node surgery and mastectomy; sometimes drained in clinic.
  • Numbness of the breast skin or the upper inner arm after node surgery, which may be permanent.
  • Lymphoedema (swelling of the arm): uncommon after sentinel node biopsy, a more significant risk after full axillary clearance.
  • Changes in breast shape, asymmetry, fat necrosis or indentation, particularly after radiotherapy.
  • With perforator flaps, partial loss of the moved tissue is uncommon but possible.
  • Bleeding, infection, delayed healing, blood clots and anaesthetic risks.

Questions patients ask

Will I need a mastectomy?

Many women do not. With oncoplastic techniques such as therapeutic mammoplasty and perforator flap reconstruction, cancers that would once have meant losing the breast can often be removed while keeping it. Whether that is safe for you depends on the size, position and type of cancer.

How soon after diagnosis is surgery?

Once the multidisciplinary team has reviewed your results and you have decided on a plan, surgery can usually be arranged within a few weeks. If other treatment such as chemotherapy is recommended first, the sequence is explained.

Will I need radiotherapy?

Radiotherapy is usually recommended after breast-conserving surgery to reduce the chance of the cancer returning in the breast, and sometimes after mastectomy. Your oncologist advises on this, and the surgery is planned with it in mind.

What about the other breast?

If surgery changes the size or shape of one breast, the other can be adjusted to match with a reduction, uplift or augmentation, either during the same operation or once treatment is complete.

Can I have reconstruction at the same time as a mastectomy?

Often, yes: immediate reconstruction with an implant or, with plastic surgery colleagues, your own tissue. Sometimes delaying reconstruction until after radiotherapy gives a better result. See the reconstruction page.

Is private breast cancer surgery covered by insurance?

Cancer surgery and treatment are usually covered by private medical insurance, subject to your policy and pre-authorisation. Miss Williams also treats NHS patients at Great Western Hospital and can discuss the options if you are considering moving between the two.
