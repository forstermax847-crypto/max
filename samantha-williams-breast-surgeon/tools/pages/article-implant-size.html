Cosmetic surgery

Choosing the right breast implant size

The question every augmentation patient asks, and how it is actually decided in clinic.

Miss Samantha Williams, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon. About 5 minutes to read.

{{draft_notice}}

Implant size is the decision patients worry about most before augmentation, and the one most often got wrong when decided alone. A well-chosen implant looks as though the breast was always that shape. A badly chosen one, usually too large, shows in every outfit and causes problems years later. This is how the decision is made in clinic.

Cup sizes are not a useful measure

Cup sizes vary a great deal between brands. A D cup in one shop is a C or an E in another. A cup size also describes the difference between underbust and bust measurements, so the same implant produces a different cup on a narrow chest than on a broad one. Surgeons think in millilitres of volume and millimetres of width. The useful conversation is about how you want to look, not about a letter.

Your frame sets the limits

The most important measurement is the width of your breast, which is determined by the width of your chest. An implant wider than the breast's natural footprint sits out towards the armpit or into the cleavage and looks unnatural. It also stretches the tissue, which thins the skin over time and can lead to rippling, visible implant edges and the breast dropping. For every patient there is a range of implant widths, and therefore volumes, that the frame will carry well. Within that range there is real choice. Outside it, the choice is being made against the anatomy.

The second factor is how much breast tissue you have to cover the implant. A slim patient with little natural tissue needs a more conservative size, and usually benefits from the implant being placed partly beneath the chest muscle so that its upper edge is not visible.

Try sizers before you decide

At the consultation you try implant sizers, silicone shapes of different volumes, inside a bra and under a fitted top, and look in a mirror. This is more useful than any photograph of someone else, because it shows the volume on your own frame. Many patients find that the size they had in mind looks larger on them than they expected and choose something more modest. A few go the other way. Either is fine. The aim is to decide by looking rather than by number.

Why slightly smaller is often the better choice

When women look back on augmentation years later, regret is far more often about having gone too large than too small. Larger implants are heavier, which speeds up the natural drooping of the breast. They are more likely to show rippling or edges, more likely to get in the way of exercise, and more obviously surgical. A result that looks natural in a swimsuit and in a work shirt is the one most people are glad of ten years on. If your surgeon suggests the smaller of two sizes you are considering, it is worth listening.

Shape and profile

Volume is only part of it. Implants come in different profiles (how far they project forward for a given width) and in round or anatomical (teardrop) shapes. A round, higher-profile implant gives more upper fullness. An anatomical implant gives a more gently sloped upper breast. Which suits you depends on your existing shape and the look you describe.

If the breast has dropped

An implant adds volume but does not lift. If the nipple sits low or the breast has deflated and dropped after children or weight loss, a larger implant will make that more obvious rather than correcting it. In that situation an uplift, with or without an implant, is the appropriate recommendation.

Questions to bring to your consultation

  • What range of sizes suits my frame, and where does the size I have in mind fall?
  • Would you place the implant over or under the muscle for me, and why?
  • Round or anatomical: what difference would it make on me?
  • Do I need an uplift as well?
  • What will this size mean for exercise, and in ten years' time?
