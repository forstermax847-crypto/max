What to expect at a breast lump assessment

What a one-stop assessment involves, step by step, and when you will have answers.

Miss Samantha Williams, Consultant Oncoplastic Breast Surgeon. About 5 minutes to read.

Draft for clinical review. This article was prepared for Miss Williams to review and approve before publication.

If you have found a lump or noticed a change in your breast, the most useful thing to know first is that most breast lumps are benign. Cysts, fibroadenomas and normal glandular tissue account for most of what is seen in clinic. The only way to be sure is to have it assessed, and the sooner that happens, the sooner the worry is dealt with.

Triple assessment

Breast clinics in the UK work to a standard called triple assessment. A lump is assessed in three ways: by a clinician, by imaging, and, if needed, by taking a sample of tissue. The three results are read together. Each test alone can miss things; combined, they are highly accurate. A private clinic follows the same standard as an NHS clinic. The main differences are the speed of the appointment and seeing the same consultant throughout.

Before the appointment

You do not need a GP referral for a self-paid appointment. If you are using insurance, your insurer will usually want one. Bring any previous breast imaging reports, a list of your medications, and a note of when you first noticed the change. Wearing separates rather than a dress makes examination easier. You are welcome to bring someone with you.

Step one: history and examination

The appointment starts with questions: what you have noticed and when, whether it changes with your cycle, any pain or nipple discharge, your general health, medications and family history. Then both breasts and the lymph nodes under the arms and in the neck are examined. A chaperone is always offered. This takes ten to fifteen minutes and tells an experienced surgeon a good deal, but it is never the whole answer, which is why imaging follows.

Step two: imaging

The type of scan depends mainly on your age, because breast tissue changes over time. In younger women the breast is denser and ultrasound shows lumps more clearly. From about forty, a mammogram is usually added. The imaging is done by specialist breast radiologists in the hospital's imaging department, and in a one-stop clinic it is done on the same day. Ultrasound is painless. A mammogram involves brief compression that most people find uncomfortable rather than painful.

Step three: biopsy, if needed

Many lumps can be diagnosed on imaging alone. A simple cyst, for example, has a characteristic appearance and can be drained there and then. If the imaging shows something that needs further characterising, a core biopsy is taken: after local anaesthetic, a needle guided by ultrasound removes a small sample of tissue. It takes a few minutes. Most people describe pressure and a clicking sound rather than pain. Some bruising afterwards is normal.

Results

Imaging results are explained before you leave. Biopsy results come back from the laboratory in about a week and are given to you in person, or by phone if you prefer, by the surgeon who saw you. If everything is benign, you will be told what, if anything, to keep an eye on. If a benign lump is large, growing or bothering you, it can be removed. If the result is cancer, you hear it from the person who will treat you, with a plan and time to understand it.

Questions worth asking

  • What did the scan show, and how confident are you in that?
  • If a biopsy is needed, when and how will I get the result?
  • Is there anything I should look out for in the meantime?
  • If this is benign, do I need any follow-up?
  • How should I check my breasts from now on?

One piece of advice

Do not wait to see whether a lump goes away. Most turn out to be nothing to worry about, but the ones that are not are easier to treat when found early. If something has changed, have it looked at.
