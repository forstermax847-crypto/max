Choosing the right breast implant size

"What size should I go for?" is the question every augmentation patient asks. The honest answer is that your body has already narrowed the choice more than you might think.

By Miss Samantha WilliamsConsultant Oncoplastic Breast SurgeonAbout 5 minutes

Draft for clinical review. This article was prepared for Miss Williams to review and approve before publication.

Implant size is the decision patients worry about most before augmentation, and the one they most often get wrong when they decide it alone. Chosen well, an implant disappears into a body that simply looks as if it was always that shape. Chosen badly — usually too large — it announces itself in every outfit and brings problems years later. Here is how the decision is actually made in clinic.

Forget cup sizes

Cup sizes are not a reliable way to describe what you want, for two reasons. First, they vary enormously between brands — a D in one shop is a C or an E in another. Second, a cup size describes the difference between your underbust and bust measurements, so the same implant produces a different "cup" on a narrow chest than on a broad one. Surgeons think in millilitres of volume and in millimetres of width, and the useful conversation is about how you want to look, not what letter you want to buy.

Your frame sets the limits

The most important measurement in choosing an implant is the width of your breast, which is set by the width of your chest. An implant wider than the breast's natural footprint will sit out towards the armpit or into the cleavage and look unnatural; it also stretches the tissue, which over time thins the skin and can lead to rippling, visible implant edges and the breast dropping. For every patient there is a range of implant widths — and therefore volumes — that the frame will carry gracefully. Within that range there is genuine choice; outside it, the choice is being made against your anatomy.

The second factor is how much breast tissue you have to cover the implant. A slim patient with little natural tissue needs a more conservative approach, and usually benefits from the implant being placed partly beneath the chest muscle, so the upper edge of the implant is softened rather than visible.

Try before you decide

At consultation you will try implant sizers — silicone shapes of different volumes — inside a bra, under a fitted top, so that you can see the effect in a mirror. This is far more useful than any photograph of someone else, because it shows the volume on your own frame. Many patients find the size they had in mind looks larger on them than they expected, and settle on something more modest; a few go the other way. Either is fine. The point is to decide with your eyes rather than with a number.

Why "a little smaller" is often the right call

There is a pattern in how women feel about augmentation years later: regret is far more often about having gone too large than too small. Larger implants are heavier, which accelerates the natural drooping of the breast; they are more likely to show rippling or edges, more likely to interfere with exercise, and more obviously "done". A result that looks natural in a swimsuit and in a work shirt is the one most people are glad of at forty-five. If your surgeon suggests the smaller of two sizes you are considering, it is worth listening — and a good surgeon will tell you honestly, even if it is not what you hoped to hear.

Shape and profile matter too

Volume is only part of the picture. Implants come in different profiles — how far they project forward for a given width — and in round or anatomical (teardrop) shapes. A round, higher-profile implant gives more upper fullness; an anatomical implant gives a more gently sloped, natural upper breast. Which suits you depends on your existing shape and the look you describe, and it is a decision made with your surgeon rather than from a catalogue.

If the breast has dropped

Finally, an implant adds volume but does not lift. If your nipple sits low or the breast has deflated and dropped after children or weight loss, a larger implant will not fix that — it will make it more obvious. In that situation an uplift, with or without an implant, is the honest recommendation, and the consultation is the place to hear it.

Questions to bring to your consultation

  • What range of sizes will suit my frame, and where does the one I have in mind fall?
  • Would you place the implant over or under the muscle for me, and why?
  • Round or anatomical — what difference would it make on me?
  • Do I need an uplift as well?
  • What will this size mean for exercise, and in ten years' time?
